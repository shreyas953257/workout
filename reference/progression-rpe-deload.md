## RPE / RIR scale

RPE (rate of perceived exertion) and RIR (reps in reserve) are the same information from two directions. **RIR is easier to judge** — "how many more clean reps could I have done?" is a more concrete question than "how hard was that?"

| RPE | RIR | Description | Where it's used |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0 | No reps left. Form broke down or nearly did. | Almost never. Occasionally on a final isolation set |
| 9.5 | 0 | Couldn't have done another rep, but might have added 1–2 kg | Testing days |
| 9 | 1 | One more clean rep available | Hardest working sets; final week of a block |
| 8.5 | 1–2 | Definitely one more, maybe two | Late in a building block |
| 8 | 2 | Two more reps, form stays clean | **The default for most hard sets** |
| 7.5 | 2–3 | Two or three more | Early weeks of a block |
| 7 | 3 | Three more, comfortable and controlled | Main-lift back-off sets, week 1–2 |
| 6 | 4+ | Several more in the tank | Deloads, technique work, ramp sets |
| ≤5 | — | Easy. Warm-up territory | Ramp-up sets only |

### How to actually estimate RIR

You learn it by occasionally taking a set close to failure on a **safe** lift (machine press, row, curl, leg press, RDL — never a heavy squat or deadlift) and comparing what you *thought* you had left with what was actually there. Most people's RPE 7 is really an RPE 5, and their RPE 9 is really a 10.

Practical anchors:
- The bar speed **slows visibly** on the last rep → roughly RPE 8–9
- Every rep moved the same speed → RPE 7 or below
- You had to **grind, rest mid-set, or break form** → RPE 10, and the set was too heavy

### Rules of thumb

- **80–90% of your sets should be RPE 7–9.** Outside that band you're either wasting the set or burying yourself.
- **Compounds: stay at RPE ≤ 9.** A missed squat rep costs you the set, your knee, and possibly your week.
- **Isolation and machines: RPE 9–10 is fine.** Nothing falls on you, and these lifts need the extra stimulus to be worth doing.
- **Week 1 of a block should feel too easy.** If your first week is RPE 9, you have no room to progress and you'll stall by week 3.

## Progression models

### 1. Linear progression — add load

Hit all prescribed reps at or below the target RPE → add weight next session.

```
Week 1:  Squat 3 × 5 @ 60 kg, RPE 7
Week 2:  Squat 3 × 5 @ 62.5 kg, RPE 7.5
Week 3:  Squat 3 × 5 @ 65 kg, RPE 8
Week 4:  Squat 3 × 5 @ 67.5 kg, RPE 8.5
```

Used in [Beginner Full-Body](../programs/beginner-full-body-3day.md). Increments: **+2.5 kg lower body, +1.25–2.5 kg upper body.** Fractional plates (0.5–1.25 kg) are worth owning — they extend linear progress on pressing lifts by months.

### 2. Double progression — add reps, then load

You're given a rep *range*. Add reps until every set hits the top, then increase the load and reset to the bottom of the range.

```
Range: 3 × 8–12

Wk 1:  28 kg × 8, 8, 8
Wk 2:  28 kg × 10, 9, 9
Wk 3:  28 kg × 12, 11, 10
Wk 4:  28 kg × 12, 12, 12   ← top of range on all sets
Wk 5:  30 kg × 9, 8, 8      ← load up, reps reset
```

Used in [Intermediate Upper/Lower](../programs/intermediate-upper-lower-4day.md) and the [home program](../programs/home-minimal-equipment.md).

### 3. Top set + back-off

One heavy set at the target RPE, then 2–3 sets at ~10% lighter. Gives you a single clean number to progress against each week without accumulating crushing fatigue. Progress it by load *or* by RPE — 100 kg @ 8 becoming 100 kg @ 7 is real progress even though the weight didn't move.

### 4. Mechanical progression

When you can't add load — bodyweight and home training — you make the exercise harder: a longer lever, a more demanding variation, a slower tempo, a bigger range of motion, or one limb instead of two. See the [variation ladders](../programs/home-minimal-equipment.md#variation-ladders).

### Which to use

| Situation | Model |
|---|---|
| Under ~1 year of consistent training | Linear |
| 1–3 years, adding weight every session has stopped | Double progression + top sets |
| Bodyweight / fixed equipment | Mechanical + double progression |
| Experienced, chasing a specific lift | Periodised block — beyond this repo's scope, but the top-set/back-off scheme here is the entry point |

## Stalling

Missing reps is a normal, expected part of training. What matters is the response. Work down this list in order and stop as soon as progress resumes.

### Step 1 — Repeat the weight

One bad session is noise: sleep, food, stress, a cold coming on, or just an off day. Attempt the same weight and reps next session. **Do not lower the weight after one miss.** Roughly half of "stalls" resolve here.

### Step 2 — Repeat again

Two misses in a row and it's a genuine stall. Check [recovery](#recovery-the-boring-stuff) first — sleep under 7 h, protein under ~1.6 g/kg, or a large calorie deficit will stall anyone on any program, and no programming cleverness fixes them.

### Step 3 — Reduce reps, hold the load

Drop the target from 3 × 5 to 3 × 3 at the same weight. Build back to 3 × 5 over two weeks, then push the load again. Keeps the weight on the bar and the movement practised.

### Step 4 — Add volume elsewhere

Sometimes the answer isn't more intensity but more practice. Add one set to that lift, or a second weekly exposure (e.g. a light technique day of 3 × 5 at 70%).

### Step 5 — Reset

Stuck for 3+ weeks? Take **10% off** and climb back, adding each session. You'll reach the old weight in 3–4 weeks and usually push straight past it. This works because it clears accumulated fatigue and rebuilds the movement at a weight you can handle cleanly.

**A reset is not failure.** People who refuse to reset stay stuck for months. People who reset break through in weeks.

### Step 6 — Change something bigger

Only after steps 1–5, and only if you've been stuck for 6–8 weeks:

- Rotate the main lift for a close variant (bench → close-grip or paused bench)
- Change the rep range (5s → 3s, or 8s → 12s)
- Increase the training frequency for that lift
- Take a full week off if you're fried — you will not lose meaningful strength in seven days

## Deloads

A planned easy week. Same exercises, roughly half the sets, ~80% of the load, RPE 6, nothing near failure. Sessions take 30–40 minutes and you leave feeling undertrained.

**What it's for:** fatigue accumulates faster than fitness. Strength and muscle adaptations lag weeks behind the work that caused them. A deload lets fatigue drop while fitness stays put — so you come back and express more than you could before. This is the actual mechanism of progress, not a rest day dressed up.

### Scheduled deloads

Built into every program here:

| Program | Deload weeks |
|---|---|
| Beginner Full-Body (12 wk) | Weeks 5 and 10 |
| Intermediate Upper/Lower (8 wk) | Weeks 4 and 8 |
| Home Minimal-Equipment | Every third block (week 13ish), or when the signs below appear |

### Unplanned deload — take one if 3+ of these hold for a week

- Lifts down 5%+ across two or more consecutive sessions
- Sleeping badly despite being tired, or waking unrefreshed
- **Joint** pain (achy shoulders, elbows, knees) — distinct from muscle soreness
- Resting heart rate noticeably above your normal
- You dread the gym rather than looking forward to it
- Appetite gone, or you've caught a cold
- Motivation is fine but the weights simply won't move

### How to deload

| Variable | Change |
|---|---|
| Sets | Halve them (3 → 2, or 4 → 2) |
| Load | ~80% of your last hard week |
| RPE | 6 — nothing hard |
| Exercises | Keep them the same. Don't swap in "fun" movements |
| Accessories | Cut to 1–2 sets or drop entirely |
| Cardio | Keep it, easy only |
| Duration | One week. Two if you're genuinely battered |

### Coming back

Resume at ~90% of your pre-deload weights and rebuild over one to two weeks. You will typically exceed your old numbers within two to three sessions.

**What not to do:** test a max during a deload week · do "just one hard set" · skip the deload because you feel fine and then miss three weeks to a niggle · extend the deload to a month because it's comfortable.

## Recovery: the boring stuff

These will move your training more than any program change. In rough order of impact:

### Sleep

**7–9 hours.** The single most important variable. Under-slept lifters produce less force, recover more slowly, report higher RPE for the same load, and lose more lean mass and more fat-free tissue when dieting. One bad night is survivable; a bad fortnight will stall you.

Practical: consistent bedtime, no screens in the last hour, room cool and dark, caffeine off by early afternoon.

### Protein

**~1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight per day.** Below that, recovery and muscle gain slow. Above ~2.2 g/kg there's no additional muscle-building benefit, though it helps satiety when dieting.

Spread across 3–5 meals of 30–50 g. Whole foods first; powder is a convenience, not a requirement.

### Calories

| Goal | Intake |
|---|---|
| Build muscle | Slight surplus, +200–300 kcal/day. Faster than that just adds fat |
| Lose fat | Moderate deficit, −300–500 kcal/day. Faster than that costs muscle and training performance |
| Recomposition / maintain | Maintenance calories. Works well for beginners and returning lifters |

**Don't run a large deficit and expect to add weight to the bar.** It can happen for a few months in beginners; after that, pick a phase.

### Everything else

- **Alcohol** — meaningfully impairs recovery and sleep quality. Occasional is fine; nightly isn't
- **Stress** — high life stress predicts both slower strength gains and higher injury risk. It's a training variable, not a separate thing from training
- **Hydration** — 2–3% dehydration measurably reduces performance. Drink through the day, not just at the gym
- **Rest days** — muscles grow when you're not training. A 4-day program needs its 3 rest days
- **Consistency over intensity** — 80% effort for two years beats 100% effort for two months, every single time

## Plateau troubleshooting

Work through this in order. Most "plateaus" are solved at steps 1–3.

| # | Check | Question | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Recovery** | Sleeping 7+ h? Eating enough protein and calories? | Fix that first. Nothing else works while this is broken |
| 2 | **Effort** | Are your sets actually RPE 8, or RPE 6 in disguise? | Take one safe set close to failure and recalibrate |
| 3 | **Progression scheme** | Are you adding load or reps when you hit targets? | Follow [§Progression models](#progression-models) — you may simply not be progressing systematically |
| 4 | **Fatigue** | Been running hard for 8+ weeks without a deload? | Deload |
| 5 | **Technique** | Are you losing reps to form breakdown rather than strength? | Film a set. Usually a bracing, bar-path, or setup issue |
| 6 | **Volume** | Recovering fine but not growing? | Add 2–4 sets per muscle group per week, one at a time |
| 7 | **Volume (too much)** | Always sore, joints achy, lifts trending down? | *Cut* volume. Over-reaching is common in intermediates who keep adding sets |
| 8 | **Exercise selection** | Been doing the same lifts for 6+ months? | Rotate main lifts for close variants |
| 9 | **Frequency** | Training a muscle once a week? | Twice weekly beats once weekly for most people at equal volume |
| 10 | **Programme** | All the above handled, 3+ months stuck? | Change program entirely — new rep ranges, new split, new stimulus |
| 11 | **Health** | Unexplained fatigue, low mood, poor sleep, low libido alongside stalled lifts? | See a doctor. Worth checking |

### How to tell progress is really happening

Don't judge on a single session. Judge on:

- **4-week trend in top-set load × reps** for your main lifts
- **Reps at the same load and RPE** — 5s @ RPE 8 becoming 6s @ RPE 8 is progress
- **Bodyweight trend** (if gaining muscle or losing fat is the goal)
- **Measurements and photos**, monthly, same conditions
- **How you feel** — better energy, less joint ache, faster recovery

Log all of it. Feelings lie; a four-week moving average of your squat doesn't.
